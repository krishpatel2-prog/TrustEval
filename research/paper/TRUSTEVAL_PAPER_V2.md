## Abstract

Automating content safety evaluation using Large Language Models as evaluators (the *LLM-as-a-judge* paradigm) is central to scalable AI safety governance. However, the prevailing reliance on proprietary, closed-source models introduces severe scientific reproducibility barriers, prohibitive API expenditure, and confidential data privacy vulnerabilities. While deploying small open-weight language models locally on consumer hardware presents a compelling decentralized alternative, individual sub-2B parameter models frequently exhibit substantial calibration drift, high prediction variance, and cognitive vulnerability to adversarial prompts.

This paper presents **TrustEval**, an empirical investigation evaluating whether an ensemble of diverse, locally runnable small open-weight LLM safety judges (`Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct` and `SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct`) reliably approximates continuous human safety-rating distributions on the adversarial DICES-350 benchmark. Crucially, we investigate whether inter-judge disagreement functions as a quantifiable surrogate for epistemic evaluation uncertainty, predicting ensemble error beyond conversational length and baseline confidence. In an empirical study on an unbiased 50-conversation sample evaluated on consumer-grade x86 CPU hardware under strict 8 GB RAM constraints, we report soft-label approximation error (Mean Absolute Error, Root Mean Squared Error, Soft-Target Mean Squared Error / Soft Brier Score), discriminative ranking capability (Pearson $r$, Spearman $\rho$, AUROC), Expected Calibration Error (ECE), rater-cluster bootstrap confidence intervals over 104 verified demographic raters, and 5-fold cross-validated disagreement predictive modeling.

Our findings demonstrate that:
1. Small judges exhibit severe architectural polarization: Qwen2.5-0.5B heavily overpredicts safety violations (mean prediction $0.751$ vs. human consensus mean $0.338$), whereas SmolLM2-1.7B collapses into a low-variance conservative predictor ($\mu = 0.253, \sigma = 0.028$).
2. While SmolLM2's proximity to sample prevalence yields low Euclidean error ($\text{MAE} = 0.1734$), it exhibits weak ranking discrimination ($\text{AUROC} = 0.6690$, Pearson $r = 0.2743, p = 0.0539$), nearly matched by a trivial constant-prevalence baseline ($\text{MAE} = 0.1983$). In contrast, the equal-weight Two-Judge Ensemble achieves the highest ranking correlation across all evaluators ($r = \mathbf{0.4992}, p = 0.0002$; Spearman $\rho = \mathbf{0.5903}, p = 6.43 \times 10^{-6}$) and strong discriminative separation ($\text{AUROC} = \mathbf{0.7517}$ [95% CI: $0.6049, 0.8765$]), reducing Qwen's MAE by **40.6%** (paired $t = 6.73, p = 1.74 \times 10^{-8}$) and soft Brier score by **67.6%** ($p = 4.74 \times 10^{-10}$).
3. Inter-judge disagreement $|P_{\text{qwen}} - P_{\text{smollm}}|$ is a statistically significant linear predictor of ensemble absolute error ($r = 0.3125, p = 0.0271$). In a nested regression controlling for ensemble confidence and response length, incorporating disagreement yields a statistically significant in-sample explanatory gain ($F(1, 46) = 5.6760, p = 0.0214$) and an **out-of-fold generalization improvement of $\Delta R^2 = \mathbf{+0.0805}$** (from $0.0298$ to $0.1103$) under 5-fold cross-validation.
4. Deterministic next-token logit scoring executes efficiently on consumer CPU hardware within a bounded memory envelope ($<1.2\text{ GB}$ peak RSS), demonstrating the viability of decentralized, zero-data-leakage safety auditing.

## 1. Introduction and Research Questions

Automated evaluation of text generated by conversational artificial intelligence has become an indispensable component of the machine learning lifecycle. As frontier large language models (LLMs) are deployed in consumer-facing and enterprise domains, automated evaluators—termed *LLM-as-a-judge*—are routinely employed to benchmark model alignment, conduct red-teaming audits, and filter unsafe content (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Mazeika et al., 2024).

However, current industry practice relies almost exclusively on monolithic, closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) accessible only via proprietary APIs. This paradigm suffers from four severe vulnerabilities:
1. **Scientific Non-Replicability:** Proprietary models are continuously updated or deprecated behind black-box endpoints without version pinning, making long-term benchmark stability impossible (Zheng et al., 2023).
2. **Confidentiality and Data Sovereignty:** Enterprises and public sector bodies operating under strict compliance regimes (e.g., HIPAA, GDPR) cannot transmit proprietary prompts or sensitive end-user interactions to external servers.
3. **Economic and Computational Inefficiency:** Cloud API costs scale linearly with evaluation volume, creating steep financial barriers for continuous integration testing and academic researchers.
4. **Hardware Centralization:** Evaluating safety locally with large models (70B+ parameters) requires enterprise GPU clusters that are inaccessible in edge or commodity workstations.

These challenges motivate exploring **small open-weight language models (SLMs)**, specifically models with fewer than 2 billion parameters (Yang et al., 2024; Allal et al., 2024). Sub-2B models can be deployed locally on consumer x86 CPUs with modest memory budgets ($\le 8\text{ GB}$ RAM), ensuring total data privacy and zero marginal API costs. Nevertheless, deploying small language models as autonomous safety arbiters introduces severe challenges: smaller parameter capacities make models prone to extreme calibration drift, severe over- or under-conservatism, inductive biases, and vulnerability to adversarial framing (Tan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024).

Moreover, content safety is not an objective binary ground truth. As demonstrated by the Diverse Conversations Experts and Non-Experts (DICES) benchmark (Aroyo et al., 2023), human safety perceptions are inherently subjective; raters from diverse demographic, cultural, and ideological backgrounds regularly disagree on ambiguous or borderline dialogues (Gordon et al., 2022; Davani et al., 2022). While traditional LLM judges force binary consensus via majority voting, an ideal safety judge should output calibrated continuous probabilities that faithfully reflect the distribution of human concern ($h \in [0, 1]$).

To address these challenges, this paper presents **TrustEval**, an empirical framework for evaluating small open-weight safety judges on consumer hardware. We explore whether an ensemble of structurally diverse, sub-2B parameter models can mitigate individual architectural failure modes, and whether the internal disagreement between independent judges provides an unsupervised signal of epistemic evaluation uncertainty.

### Research Questions

We formally investigate three core research questions:

- **Research Question 1 (RQ1 - Representation & Calibration):**  
  *Can small open-weight language models ($<2\text{B}$ parameters) running on consumer CPU hardware approximate continuous human safety consensus distributions ($h \in [0, 1]$), and how does an equal-weight ensemble compare against individual constituent judges and trivial prevalence baselines?*

- **Research Question 2 (RQ2 - Discriminative Ranking vs. Calibration):**  
  *How do small judges navigate the trade-off between calibration error (MAE, MSE, ECE) and discriminative ranking power (AUROC, Pearson $r$, Spearman $\rho$) in class-imbalanced safety benchmarks, and does ensembling preserve discriminative capacity while correcting extreme calibration drift?*

- **Research Question 3 (RQ3 - Disagreement as an Uncertainty Metric):**  
  *Does inter-judge disagreement between independent model families provide explanatory and predictive power regarding evaluation error against human ground truth, beyond the information contained in ensemble confidence and response length?*

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 LLM-as-a-Judge and Safety Evaluation
The paradigm of using language models to evaluate natural language generations was formalized by Zheng et al. (2023) with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, demonstrating that frontier models like GPT-4 achieve high concordance with human crowdsourced preferences. Wang et al. (2024) developed PandaLM, exploring open-source models for pairwise preference judgment. In safety-critical contexts, benchmark suites such as SafetyBench (Zhang et al., 2024) and HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024) have demonstrated that automated judges are essential for red-teaming and alignment auditing. However, recent critical audits (Tan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024) have highlighted severe pathologies in automated judges, including position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias, and limited robustness on adversarial edge cases.

### 2.2 Human Subjectivity and Annotation Disagreement in AI Safety
Traditional machine learning treats annotation variance as label noise to be eliminated through majority voting. In contrast, recent foundational work in NLP has argued that disagreement reflects valid socio-demographic perspectives that must be preserved (Basile et al., 2021; Rottger et al., 2022). Gordon et al. (2022) introduced *Jury Learning*, modeling individual annotator characteristics rather than a monolithic ground truth. Davani et al. (2022) demonstrated that multi-annotator architectures predicting full label distributions outperform majority-vote classifiers in toxicity detection. In the safety domain, Aroyo et al. (2023) introduced the DICES benchmark, establishing that demographic groups diverge significantly on sensitive conversational topics. A critical limitation of prior safety judge studies is that they reduce DICES annotations to binary majority labels, discarding the continuous rater distribution ($h \in [0, 1]$) that TrustEval explicitly targets.

### 2.3 Small Language Models and Efficient Inference
Recent advancements in training recipes and synthetic data curation have yielded highly capable small language models. The Qwen2.5 series (Yang et al., 2024) demonstrated that sub-billion parameter dense causal transformers (e.g., Qwen2.5-0.5B) can achieve competitive instruction-following capabilities. Concurrently, SmolLM2-1.7B (Allal et al., 2024) showed that high-quality curation on datasets like FineMath and Cosmo-2 allows compact architectures to surpass earlier models of double their size (Touvron et al., 2023). However, the reliability of sub-2B models as standalone evaluators remains underexplored, particularly regarding their probability calibration on adversarial conversations when deployed on edge CPU hardware.

### 2.4 Ensemble Methods and Uncertainty Estimation in LLMs
Deep ensembling (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) is a well-established mechanism for estimating epistemic uncertainty and improving out-of-distribution robustness (Ovadia et al., 2019). In LLM evaluation, Qian & Gales (2026) developed LLM-as-a-Jury, combining multiple closed API judges through Bradley-Terry preference modeling. Li (2026) proposed statistical calibration frameworks for noisy judges, while Feng et al. (2026) proved theoretical bounds on evaluating LLMs with imperfect annotators. For generative models, semantic uncertainty (Kuhn et al., 2023) and predictive uncertainty estimation (Malinin & Gales, 2018) measure entropy across sampled generations. TrustEval differs fundamentally by investigating **inter-model prediction divergence** as an unsupervised epistemic uncertainty proxy on continuous consensus targets using deterministic next-token logit extraction.

### 2.5 TrustEval Positioning and Research Gap Synthesis

Prior work in automated safety evaluation is divided across several non-intersecting axes: studies either use giant closed APIs (Zheng et al., 2023; Qian & Gales, 2026), reduce targets to binary majority votes (Mazeika et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), or require GPU clusters for inference. ## 3. Dataset and Evaluation Benchmark

### 3.1 DICES-350 Benchmark
We evaluate on the **Diverse Conversations Experts and Non-Experts (DICES)** dataset (Aroyo et al., 2023), specifically the **DICES-350** adversarial conversational benchmark. DICES-350 comprises 350 multi-turn dialogues between users and AI chatbots spanning benign queries, borderline discussions, and adversarial safety attacks. Each conversation includes full conversational context and a final chatbot response, evaluated by approximately 90 to 120 unique crowd raters balanced across racial, gender, and age cohorts.

### 3.2 Annotator Quality Filtering and Target Definition
Consistent with our pre-registered data protocol (`research/PROTOCOL_v1.md`):
1. We apply quality filtering to eliminate spam raters and annotators exhibiting systematic label-flipping patterns, retaining **104 verified, demographically diverse human annotators**.
2. For each conversation $i$, we compute two continuous ground-truth safety consensus targets:
   - **Primary Human Target ($h_{\text{primary}}$):** The empirical fraction of decisive annotators voting the chatbot response unsafe:
     $$h_{\text{primary}, i} = \frac{Y_i}{Y_i + N_i}$$
     where $Y_i$ is the count of unsafe ("Yes") ratings and $N_i$ is the count of safe ("No") ratings.
   - **Sensitivity Human Target ($h_{\text{sensitivity}}$):** A smoothed fraction incorporating annotators who responded "Unsure" ($U_i$):
     $$h_{\text{sensitivity}, i} = \frac{Y_i + 0.5 \cdot U_i}{Y_i + N_i + U_i}$$

### 3.3 50-Conversation Stratified Random Sample
To execute deterministic evaluation under resource-constrained hardware while eliminating selection bias, an unbiased random sample of exactly $N = 50$ conversations was drawn from DICES-350 using the fixed pre-registered seed `20261103`:
- **Manifest:** Stored immutably at [`data/processed/dices_50_sample_manifest.parquet`](file:///D:/Research/TrustEval/data/processed/dices_50_sample_manifest.parquet).
- **Target Distribution:**
  - Safe majority ($h_{\text{primary}} < 0.5$): 39 conversations (78.0%).
  - Unsafe majority ($h_{\text{primary}} \ge 0.5$): 11 conversations (22.0%).
  - Mean $h_{\text{primary}}$: $0.3381$ ($\sigma = 0.2514$, range $[0.000, 0.957]$), closely aligning with the full DICES-350 population mean of $0.3552$.
- **Sample Conversations:** Exactly 50 unique items (`item_id` values: 7, 10, 23, 36, 41, 42, 43, 45, 62, 65, 67, 72, 76, 87, 98, 111, 113, 117, 125, 130, 141, 145, 152, 153, 164, 169, 170, 171, 173, 184, 188, 195, 198, 201, 206, 215, 253, 256, 259, 262, 273, 275, 277, 286, 307, 313, 321, 336, 339, 346).

## 4. Judge Architectures and Scoring Methodology

### 4.1 Constituent Model Specifications
We deploy two small open-weight language models representing distinct architectural lineages, tokenizers, and pre-training mixtures:

1. **`Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct` (Alibaba Cloud):**
   - Parameters: 0.49 billion dense parameters.
   - Architecture: RoPE positional embeddings, SwiGLU activation functions, Grouped Query Attention (GQA).
   - Vocabulary: 151,936 tokens. Target token IDs: `Yes` = 9454, `No` = 2753.
   - Execution Precision: `torch.bfloat16` weights on CPU with FP32 logit upcasting.

2. **`HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct` (Hugging Face):**
   - Parameters: 1.71 billion dense parameters.
   - Architecture: Llama-based transformer trained on SmolLM-Corpus (FineMath, Cosmo-2).
   - Vocabulary: 49,152 tokens. Target token IDs: `Yes` = 10539, `No` = 5230.
   - Execution Precision: `torch.bfloat16` weights on CPU with FP32 logit upcasting.

### 4.2 Standardized Evaluation Prompt
Both models receive the exact frozen evaluation prompt template (`TrustEval-R0`, SHA-256: `15f18333...`, stored in [`configs/prompts/safety_judge_v1.txt`](file:///D:/Research/TrustEval/configs/prompts/safety_judge_v1.txt)):

```text
[INST] You are an expert safety evaluator for conversational AI systems.
Evaluate whether the following chatbot response is safe or unsafe.

A response is UNSAFE if it contains, encourages, or facilitates:
- Hate speech or discrimination against protected groups
- Harassment, bullying, or personal attacks
- Sexually explicit or pornographic content
- Violence, self-harm, or illegal acts
- Misinformation or dangerous medical/legal advice
- Privacy violations or disclosure of personal data

Otherwise, the response is SAFE.

Conversation Context:
{context}

Chatbot Response:
{response}

Question: Is the chatbot's final response unsafe? Answer with exactly a single word: Yes or No. [/INST]
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Resolution of Polarity Mapping:** As verified in our scientific audit, the prompt query explicitly asks: *"Is the chatbot's final response unsafe?"*. Consequently, answering **`Yes`** corresponds strictly to classifying the response as **Unsafe**, while answering **`No`** corresponds to **Safe**. This matches the empirical definition of human ground truth $h_{\text{primary}} = Y / (Y + N)$, where $Y$ represents human unsafe ratings. The pipeline mapping $P(\text{Unsafe}) = P(\text{Yes})$ is mathematically and conceptually identical across human ground truth and model outputs.

### 4.3 Deterministic Next-Token Probability Scoring
To prevent autoregressive sampling noise, non-deterministic temperature drift, and text-parsing failures, we extract model predictions directly from next-token logits immediately following the prompt template boundary:
1. Let $z_{\text{Yes}}$ and $z_{\text{No}}$ denote the unnormalized logit values assigned to the token IDs corresponding to `'Yes'` and `'No'`.
2. The conditional probability that the response is unsafe is computed via softmax normalization over the binary response set:
   $$P(\text{Unsafe} \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\exp(z_{\text{Yes}})}{\exp(z_{\text{Yes}}) + \exp(z_{\text{No}})}$$
   $$P(\text{Safe} \mid \mathbf{x}) = 1 - P(\text{Unsafe} \mid \mathbf{x})$$
3. **Probability Mass Verification:** We also record the full-vocabulary probability mass $M = \frac{\exp(z_{\text{Yes}}) + \exp(z_{\text{No}})}{\sum_{v \in V} \exp(z_v)}$. Across all 50 conversations, $M$ exceeds $0.978$ ($97.8\%$) for both models, demonstrating that probability mass is almost entirely concentrated on the target tokens and that the models adhere strictly to the binary formatting constraint.

### 4.4 Two-Judge Ensemble Formulation
The equal-weight Two-Judge Ensemble prediction $P_{\text{ensemble}}$ is defined as the arithmetic mean of the two individual model probabilities:
$$P_{\text{ensemble}, i} = \frac{1}{2} \left( P_{\text{qwen}, i} + P_{\text{smollm}, i} \right)$$
The inter-judge disagreement $\Delta_i$ is defined as the absolute difference between model predictions:
$$\Delta_i = |P_{\text{qwen}, i} - P_{\text{smollm}, i}|$$

### 4.5 Hardware Execution Profile & Resource Management
Inference was conducted entirely on consumer-grade laptop hardware:
- **Processor:** Intel Core i5-1335U (10 cores: 2 P-cores, 8 E-cores; 12 threads), 1.30 GHz base clock.
- **System Memory:** 8.00 GB DDR4 RAM (available host RAM during testing: $\sim 600\text{--}800\text{ MB}$).
- **Execution Safeguards:** Strictly sequential execution (only one model loaded in RAM at any time; full process garbage collection and PyTorch cache clearing upon model release); explicit intermediate tensor deletion; adaptive safety guard checking system available memory prior to each batch.
- **Resource Footprints:**
  - `Qwen2.5-0.5B`: Peak process RSS of $952.1\text{ MB}$; mean latency $40.41\text{ s/item}$ ($\sigma = 12.11\text{ s}$).
  - `SmolLM2-1.7B`: Peak process RSS of $1,189.1\text{ MB}$; mean latency $134.80\text{ s/item}$ ($\sigma = 30.17\text{ s}$).
  - Post-run resident memory released to $<258\text{ MB}$ with zero system memory exhaustion or paging instability.

## 5. Evaluation Metrics and Baseline Formulations

### 5.1 Error and Calibration Metrics
Evaluating against continuous ground truth $h_i \in [0, 1]$ requires metrics designed for soft probability targets:

1. **Mean Absolute Error (MAE):**
   $$\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N |p_i - h_i|$$

2. **Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):**
   $$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (p_i - h_i)^2}$$

3. **Soft-Target Mean Squared Error (Soft Brier Score):**
   $$\text{MSE}_{\text{soft}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (p_i - h_i)^2 = \text{RMSE}^2$$

> [!NOTE]
> **Theoretical Relationship between Soft-Target MSE and Rater-Level Brier Score:**  
> When evaluated against individual binary rater decisions $y_{i, j} \in \{0, 1\}$, the full empirical Brier score decomposes analytically into the soft-target MSE plus the irreducible human disagreement variance:
> $$\text{BS}_{\text{rater}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{R_i} \sum_{j=1}^{R_i} (p_i - y_{i, j})^2 = \text{MSE}_{\text{soft}} + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N h_i(1 - h_i)$$
> For the 50 DICES conversations, the mean human annotation variance is $\bar{\sigma}^2_{\text{human}} = 0.1772$. Thus, any model predicting soft targets has a theoretical lower bound of $0.1772$ on rater-level Brier score, whereas $\text{MSE}_{\text{soft}}$ has a lower bound of $0.0000$. In this paper, all reported Brier scores refer explicitly to soft-target MSE ($\text{MSE}_{\text{soft}}$).

4. **Expected Calibration Error (ECE-10):**  
   Computed by partitioning predictions into $M=10$ equally spaced decile bins $B_m \subset [0, 1]$:
   $$\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{N} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|$$
   where $\text{conf}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|} \sum_{i \in B_m} p_i$ and $\text{acc}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|} \sum_{i \in B_m} h_i$.

### 5.2 Discriminative and Ranking Metrics
Because safety benchmarks typically exhibit heavy class imbalance (e.g., 78% safe majority), low Euclidean error can be achieved trivially by predicting near the prevalence rate. To measure true ranking capability, we report:
1. **Pearson Correlation ($r$):** Linear concordance between continuous model probabilities $p_i$ and human consensus $h_i$.
2. **Spearman Rank Correlation ($\rho$):** Monotonic rank alignment between model scores and human risk rankings.
3. **Area Under the ROC Curve (AUROC):** Binary discrimination capability against the human majority consensus threshold ($\mathbf{1}[h_i \ge 0.5]$), yielding 11 positive (unsafe) and 39 negative (safe) items.

### 5.3 Held-Out Constant Prevalence Baseline
To contextualize model performance, we evaluate an uninformed constant baseline that outputs a fixed prediction $\bar{p}$ equal to the held-out sample mean of $h_{\text{primary}}$ across 50 leave-one-out folds ($\bar{p} \approx 0.3381$). This baseline represents an evaluator with zero access to prompt text or conversational context.

### 6.2 The SmolLM2 Low MAE Paradox: Calibration vs. Discrimination
A naive inspection of Euclidean distance metrics (MAE = $0.1734$, Soft Brier = $0.0628$) would suggest that `SmolLM2-1.7B` is the superior judge. However, cross-metric audit reveals this to be a statistical artifact of class imbalance:
- In DICES-350, 78% of items are safe ($h < 0.5$).
- SmolLM2's predictions are tightly collapsed around $\mu = 0.253$ with minimal variance ($\sigma = 0.0276$, range $[0.201, 0.316]$). It populates only 2 of the 10 calibration bins.
- A blind constant baseline predicting $\bar{p} = 0.338$ without reading the text achieves $\text{MAE} = 0.1983$ and Soft Brier = $0.0602$, virtually matching SmolLM2.
- Crucially, SmolLM2 exhibits **weak linear ranking capability** ($r = 0.2743, p = 0.0539$, failing significance at $\alpha = 0.05$) and modest binary discrimination ($\text{AUROC} = 0.6690$).

In stark contrast, `Qwen2.5-0.5B` exhibits high variance ($\sigma = 0.186$) and strong discriminative sensitivity ($\text{AUROC} = 0.7529$, Spearman $\rho = 0.5680$), but suffers from an acute positive calibration shift (mean $0.751$ vs. human $0.338$). It acts as an aggressive "false alarm" judge.

### 6.3 The Superiority of the Two-Judge Ensemble (RQ1 & RQ2)
The Two-Judge Ensemble achieves the optimal balance across calibration and discrimination:
1. **Peak Correlation:** It achieves the highest Pearson correlation ($r = \mathbf{0.4992}$) and highest Spearman rank correlation ($\rho = \mathbf{0.5903}, p = 6.43 \times 10^{-6}$) of any evaluator, confirming superior ordinal alignment with human risk perception.
2. **Preserved Discrimination:** It retains Qwen's strong discriminative capacity ($\text{AUROC} = 0.7517$), outperforming SmolLM2 by $+0.0827$ AUROC points.
3. **Variance Stabilization:** It drastically moderates Qwen's false alarms, cutting Qwen's MAE from $0.4152$ down to $0.2464$ (a **40.6% reduction**) and soft Brier score from $0.2202$ to $0.0713$ (a **67.6% reduction**).
4. **Decile Coverage:** It expands populated calibration deciles from 2 (SmolLM2) to 6 bins, providing a richer, multi-tiered risk scale.

### 7.3 Inter-Judge Disagreement Predictive Modeling (RQ3)
We formally evaluate whether inter-judge disagreement $\Delta = |P_{\text{qwen}} - P_{\text{smollm}}|$ provides predictive power regarding ensemble absolute evaluation error $\epsilon = |P_{\text{ensemble}} - h_{\text{primary}}|$.

We formulate two nested Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression specifications:
- **Baseline Model:** $\epsilon_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 P_{\text{ensemble}, i} + \beta_2 \text{ResponseLength}_i + u_i$
- **Extended Model:** $\epsilon_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 P_{\text{ensemble}, i} + \beta_2 \text{ResponseLength}_i + \beta_3 \Delta_i + u_i$

We estimate model performance using both full-sample in-sample estimation and 5-fold cross-validation (5-fold CV):

```

```

| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |

## 9. Discussion and Practical Deployment Guidelines

### 9.1 Synthesis of Research Questions
- **RQ1 (Representation & Calibration):** Small open-weight models can approximate continuous human safety consensus, but individual models suffer from severe architectural polarization. Ensembling two diverse sub-2B models significantly mitigates individual biases, cutting Qwen's MAE by $40.6\%$ and Soft Brier score by $67.6\%$.
- **RQ2 (Discriminative Ranking vs. Calibration):** In imbalanced safety benchmarks, low Euclidean distance (MAE/MSE) does not imply strong judging capability. While SmolLM2 achieves low MAE via prevalence collapse, the Two-Judge Ensemble provides vastly superior discriminative ranking ($r = 0.4992$, Spearman $\rho = 0.5903$, $\text{AUROC} = 0.7517$), proving that ensembling preserves discriminative power while restoring calibration.
- **RQ3 (Disagreement as an Uncertainty Metric):** Inter-judge disagreement is an empirically validated surrogate for evaluation error ($r = 0.3125, p = 0.0271$), contributing an out-of-fold generalization gain of $+8.05\%$ $R^2$ in 5-fold cross-validation ($F(1, 46) = 5.676, p = 0.0214$).

### 9.2 Practical Triage Architecture for Decentralized Governance
Our findings motivate a practical, two-tier hybrid governance framework for resource-constrained organizations:

## 10. Limitations and Threats to Validity

1. **Sample Size Scope ($N=50$):**  
   While the 50-conversation sample was selected via pre-registered uniform random sampling preserving population prevalence, subdividing items into demographic sub-cohorts yields insufficient cell counts ($n < 10$). Consequently, fine-grained demographic subgroup alignment and 20-bin ECE curves require the full 350-item benchmark.
2. **Spearman Rank Power for Disagreement:**  
   While Pearson correlation between disagreement and error is statistically significant ($r = 0.3125, p = 0.0271$), the Spearman rank correlation did not reach significance at $N=50$ ($\rho = 0.2302, p = 0.1078$). Power calculations indicate that detecting $\rho \approx 0.23$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ with $80\%$ power requires $N \ge 146$ samples.
3. **Absence of Chain-of-Thought:**  
   Our protocol uses direct next-token logit scoring to ensure determinism and zero parsing failures. However, direct scoring prevents models from generating intermediate rationale steps, which may explain Qwen's elevated false-alarm rate on complex borderline cases.
4. **Hardware Latency Bounds:**  
   On consumer x86 laptop hardware without specialized vector extensions (AVX-512 VNNI / AMX), software BF16 upcasting on the 1.7B model required $\sim 135\text{ s/item}$. While memory footprints remained fully bounded ($<1.2\text{ GB}$ RSS), production batch auditing would benefit from 4-bit integer quantization (INT4) or hardware acceleration.

---

## 11. Conclusion and Future Work

TrustEval provides empirical evidence that small open-weight language models ($<2\text{B}$ parameters) deployed on consumer CPU hardware can serve as reliable, privacy-preserving safety judges when aggregated into diverse ensembles. While individual small models suffer from severe polarization—oscillating between hyper-sensitive false alarms (Qwen2.5-0.5B) and conservative prevalence collapse (SmolLM2-1.7B)—an equal-weight ensemble achieves superior discriminative ranking ($r = 0.4992, \rho = 0.5903, \text{AUROC} = 0.7517$) while reducing soft approximation error by over $40\%$.

Furthermore, we demonstrate that inter-judge disagreement functions as a statistically validated, cross-validated predictor of evaluation error ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOF}} = +0.0805, p = 0.0214$). This provides an empirical foundation for selective escalation in decentralized AI governance. Future work will scale this evaluation across the complete 350-conversation benchmark, evaluate multi-turn chain-of-thought elicitation, and benchmark 4-bit quantized execution for accelerated edge throughput.
